## 5.1 Summary

This study set out to design, implement, and evaluate FarmBuddy, an artificial intelligence-powered agricultural advisory system tailored to the specific needs of Nigerian smallholder farmers. The motivation for the work arose from the persistent gap between the agricultural knowledge available to large-scale commercial farmers and the practical guidance accessible to the rural smallholder, who typically operates with limited formal education, restricted internet access, and little or no contact with professional extension agents. Existing digital agricultural tools were found to address this gap inadequately, owing to their reliance on stable broadband connectivity, their use of foreign languages and unfamiliar cultural contexts, and their failure to accommodate users with low literacy.

Chapter One established the research problem, articulated the aim and objectives of the study, defined the scope of the system, and identified the significance of the work for Nigerian agriculture. The study adopted a design-science research methodology, seeking to produce and evaluate an artefact — the FarmBuddy system — that demonstrably improved advisory access for the target population.

Chapter Two reviewed the relevant literature on precision agriculture, AI-assisted farming systems, natural language processing for agricultural advisory, multilingual chatbot design, plant disease detection using deep learning, and progressive web applications in developing-country contexts. The review identified the limitations of prior systems, established theoretical foundations for the chosen design approach, and confirmed that no existing system adequately combined conversational AI, multilingual speech interaction, plant disease vision diagnosis, real-time weather advisory, and cross-platform accessibility in a single, locally contextualised product.

Chapter Three presented the system design and architecture. The overall design followed a three-tier client-server model comprising a Next.js Progressive Web App frontend, a Django REST API backend, and a Telegram bot as an alternative access channel. The database schema, API architecture, AI integration model, and security design were documented in detail. The system was designed to support four Nigerian languages (English, Hausa, Igbo, and Yoruba) across all interaction channels and to function in offline or low-connectivity conditions through PWA caching.

Chapter Four documented the full implementation of the system and presented the results of functional and performance testing. The backend was implemented with Django 6.0 and Python 3.12.9, integrating the Google Gemini API (`gemini-flash-latest`) for conversational advisory, the Gemini Files API with the `gemini-2.0-flash` model for audio transcription, the Gemini Vision capability for plant disease detection, the OpenWeatherMap API for weather data, and the YarnGPT API for text-to-speech synthesis in Nigerian-accented voices. The frontend was built with Next.js 16.1.6, React 19.2.4, and Tailwind CSS, and was deployed as a Progressive Web App with Service Worker-based offline caching. The Telegram bot implemented eleven slash commands and a full sixteen-step registration flow mirroring the web interface, with database connection management implemented via a `close_old_connections()` decorator pattern to ensure reliability in the long-running asynchronous process.

Feature testing demonstrated an 85% plant disease detection accuracy across twenty test images, successful multilingual AI responses in all four target languages, and a System Usability Scale mean score of 78.3 — placing the system in the "Good" usability band. Cross-platform data synchronisation between the web application and the Telegram bot was verified across four distinct test scenarios. Performance benchmarking recorded first-token AI response latencies of 1.2 to 2.8 seconds for streaming chat responses, confirming an acceptable user experience even over mobile internet connections.

## 5.2 Conclusion

The FarmBuddy system, as designed, implemented, and evaluated in this study, successfully addresses the central research problem by providing Nigerian smallholder farmers with reliable, accessible, and contextualised agricultural advisory through multiple complementary channels. The system demonstrates that the integration of large language model technology with domain-specific agricultural context, local language support, and low-barrier access channels can meaningfully extend the reach of expert agricultural guidance to populations that have historically been underserved by both formal extension services and existing digital tools.

Several conclusions may be drawn from the research findings:

**AI-driven advisory is viable and valued in the agricultural context.** The conversational AI feature, which received the highest usefulness rating (4.7 out of 5) in the user study, demonstrated that general-purpose large language models can, when appropriately prompted with farmer-specific profile context and domain persona instructions, deliver practically applicable and culturally grounded agricultural advice. The integration of real-time weather data through the model's function-calling capability further enriched response quality by grounding recommendations in the farmer's current environmental conditions.

**Multimodal interaction significantly improves accessibility.** The combination of text, voice, and image interaction modalities addressed the heterogeneous literacy levels of the target population. Voice input, valued most strongly by lower-literacy participants, achieved transcription accuracy rates between 81% and 92% across the four target languages, sufficient for reliable command understanding in agricultural advisory contexts. The plant disease detection feature — which allowed farmers to photograph a diseased leaf and receive an immediate diagnosis — emerged as the feature generating the strongest engagement and emotional response from test participants, suggesting its potential as an entry-point feature for technology adoption.

**Progressive Web App architecture is appropriate for the Nigerian mobile context.** The PWA approach provided the installability and offline caching characteristics of a native mobile application without the barriers of an app store listing, a large download, or a separate codebase. Offline access to previously loaded conversations proved particularly relevant for farmers in areas with intermittent network connectivity, confirming that the architectural choice was well-suited to the deployment context.

**Cross-platform design via Telegram materially extends the system's reach.** By providing a fully functional version of the advisory system through the Telegram bot — an application already widely installed in Nigeria — the system reached participants who owned feature-adjacent smartphones or who were more comfortable in a familiar messaging environment than in a dedicated web application. The identical data model underpinning both platforms ensured seamless account portability.

**Multilingual support in four Nigerian languages is technically feasible and practically impactful.** Providing AI responses, UI labels, TTS voices, and agricultural tips in English, Hausa, Igbo, and Yoruba required deliberate engineering effort at every layer of the stack. The user study confirmed that language-native interaction was not merely a cosmetic feature: Hausa-speaking participants explicitly reported that receiving advice in Hausa increased the information's perceived trustworthiness and actionability, a finding consistent with prior research on language-of-instruction effects in low-literacy advisory contexts.

In summation, this study makes a demonstrable contribution to the intersection of agricultural technology and AI accessibility research by showing that a well-designed, locally contextualised AI advisory system can achieve both technical soundness and genuine user value when built with an understanding of the infrastructure, linguistic, and cultural realities of its target population.

## 5.3 Recommendations

The following recommendations are proposed to enhance the impact of FarmBuddy and inform the development of similar agricultural advisory systems in Nigeria and other developing-country contexts.

### 5.3.1 Integration with Mobile Money for Input Purchases

A significant friction point in the advisory cycle is the gap between knowing what agricultural inputs are needed and being able to procure them affordably. The FarmBuddy system currently provides advice on inputs (fertilisers, pesticides, seeds) but offers no mechanism for acquisition. It is recommended that the system be extended with integration into mobile money platforms — particularly those with strong rural penetration in Nigeria, such as Opay, PalmPay, and MTN MoMo — to allow farmers to purchase recommended inputs directly from within the application after receiving advisory guidance. This integration would close the knowledge-to-action loop and create a sustainable revenue stream that could subsidise the system's operational costs, reducing dependence on donor or grant funding.

### 5.3.2 Community Feature for Farmer Networking

Smallholder farmers derive significant value from peer learning — sharing experiences of what worked in a given season, warning neighbours about emerging pest outbreaks, and coordinating group purchases to achieve bulk-discount pricing. The current system is strictly individual. It is recommended that a community layer be introduced, comprising a moderated discussion forum or cooperative group chat feature, within which farmers in the same locality or cultivating the same crops can exchange experiences, share AI-generated advice, and form informal cooperatives. The AI could moderate discussions to surface inaccurate advice and inject verified information where peer recommendations deviate from sound agricultural practice.

### 5.3.3 Soil Testing Integration

The FarmBuddy system currently relies on user-reported soil type, pH level, and water source data, which may be inaccurate given that many smallholder farmers have never had their soil formally tested. It is recommended that the system explore integration with portable soil testing kits — increasingly available in Nigeria through initiatives such as the Soil Information System (SIS-Nigeria) — and with laboratories that offer bulk soil testing services. A workflow in which a farmer collects a soil sample, submits it to a linked laboratory, and receives the laboratory results directly in their FarmBuddy profile (auto-populating the soil fields) would substantially improve the accuracy of AI recommendations grounded in soil chemistry. This integration could be implemented through a laboratory API or through a QR code-based sample tracking system.

### 5.3.4 Market Price Information

Agricultural decision-making is inseparable from market economics. A farmer who knows that cassava prices are expected to fall at harvest due to regional oversupply should diversify to a different crop or stagger planting to hit a better price window. Currently, FarmBuddy provides no market price intelligence. It is recommended that the system be extended with integration into agricultural commodity price feeds — such as those maintained by the Commodity Markets Outlook databases, AFEX, or the Nigerian Exchange Group's agricultural commodity indices — to provide real-time and forecasted crop price information alongside agronomic advice. The AI model could then offer economically contextualised recommendations (e.g., "Given that cowpea prices have risen 12% in Kano market over the past month, consider allocating part of your fallow land to cowpea this season").

### 5.3.5 Crop Yield Prediction Models

The current advisory model is reactive: farmers ask questions and receive answers. A more proactive capability would be the prediction of expected crop yields based on the user's farm profile, current soil conditions, historical weather patterns for their location, and the planting decisions they have communicated to the system. It is recommended that domain-specific machine learning models — trained on Nigerian agricultural yield datasets such as those maintained by the National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) and the Federal Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development (FMARD) — be integrated into FarmBuddy to generate season-opening yield predictions. These predictions would enable farmers to set realistic income expectations, make informed decisions about credit uptake for input financing, and plan post-harvest storage or transportation arrangements in advance.

### 5.3.6 SMS Fallback for Feature Phones

While the Telegram bot extends accessibility beyond the web application, it still requires a smartphone and internet connectivity. A substantial portion of Nigeria's rural farming population uses feature phones capable only of SMS and voice calls. It is recommended that an SMS gateway integration — using a provider such as Termii, Africa's Talking, or Twilio — be developed to expose a simplified version of the FarmBuddy advisory system over SMS. The interaction model would necessarily be simpler than the conversational AI chat (owing to the 160-character SMS constraint), but a menu-driven system offering daily tips, a basic crop disease report mechanism (via photo-less textual symptom description), and weather alerts by SMS would extend meaningful advisory access to the most resource-constrained segment of the farming population.

## 5.4 Limitations of the Study

Despite the positive outcomes reported in this study, several limitations must be acknowledged that qualify the conclusions drawn and inform the scope within which the findings should be generalised.

**Scope of user study participants.** The usability study involved fifteen participants drawn from two geo-political zones (South-West and North-West Nigeria). The findings, while internally consistent, cannot be assumed to represent the full diversity of Nigerian farming communities, which span six geo-political zones with distinct agricultural practices, agro-ecological zones, literacy profiles, and linguistic communities. A larger-scale field study involving at minimum one hundred participants drawn across all six zones would be required before broad generalisability claims could be made.

**AI response accuracy not formally validated by agronomists.** While AI responses were reviewed informally against published Nigerian agricultural extension guidance and no factually incorrect advice was observed during the test sessions, no formal validation of advisory accuracy was conducted by credentialled agronomists or verified against a structured ground-truth dataset of Nigerian farming scenarios. Agricultural advisory carries a degree of risk — incorrect pesticide or fertiliser recommendations can cause crop damage or financial loss — and a formal expert validation study is a prerequisite before the system is deployed at population scale.

**Plant disease detection accuracy constrained by base model.** The 85% plant disease detection accuracy obtained in testing relied on the general-purpose Gemini Vision model rather than a domain-fine-tuned agricultural vision model. The test image set was limited to twenty images across four disease categories and may not adequately represent the full range of crop diseases prevalent in Nigerian farming contexts. Accuracy may degrade on images captured with lower-quality mobile cameras, under poor lighting conditions, or for less visually distinct diseases.

**Absence of longitudinal evaluation.** The evaluation conducted in this study was cross-sectional, capturing user responses after a single structured interaction session. No longitudinal data was collected on whether farmers continued to use the system after initial exposure, whether advisory uptake resulted in measurable improvements to crop yield or pest management outcomes, or how usage patterns evolved over a full growing season. Longitudinal measurement would be essential to establish whether FarmBuddy delivers genuine agricultural impact beyond user satisfaction.

**Dependency on third-party API availability.** The system's core features — AI advisory, plant disease detection, audio transcription, text-to-speech, and weather data — are all dependent on the continued availability and pricing of third-party APIs (Google Gemini, OpenWeatherMap, YarnGPT). Any disruption to these services, pricing changes, or terms-of-service modifications would directly impact system functionality. The absence of self-hosted fallback models represents a sustainability risk, particularly for a system intended to serve resource-limited communities.

**Language coverage incomplete for Nigeria's full linguistic diversity.** Nigeria is home to over 500 languages and dialects. The four languages supported by FarmBuddy (English, Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba) cover the three major language groups plus the national lingua franca, but exclude significant farming communities who speak Fulfulde, Tiv, Ijaw, Kanuri, Ibibio, and dozens of other languages. Farmers for whom none of the four supported languages is a first language receive inferior advisory quality compared to native speakers.

**Network connectivity dependency for AI features.** While PWA caching enables offline access to previously loaded content, all AI-powered features (chat, disease detection, transcription, TTS) require an active internet connection. In areas with severe connectivity constraints — including much of Nigeria's rural North-East and North-West — the system may be unusable for the populations that arguably need agricultural advisory support most acutely.

## 5.5 Suggestions for Future Work

Building on the findings, limitations, and recommendations of this study, the following directions are proposed for future research and development.

### 5.5.1 Expansion to More Nigerian Languages

Future work should prioritise the extension of FarmBuddy's language support beyond the four currently implemented languages. Of particular importance are Fulfulde (spoken by the Fulani pastoralist community, whose livestock management practices intersect significantly with crop farming through manure and land-use relationships), Tiv (widely spoken in the Benue food belt, one of Nigeria's most agriculturally productive states), and Ijaw (relevant to aquaculture and riverine farming communities in the Niger Delta). Expansion would require the collection or commissioning of training data for these languages, evaluation of Gemini's multilingual capability for each target language, collaboration with native speakers to validate translations of the agricultural tip corpus and UI labels, and the sourcing of TTS voices in the additional languages. Where high-quality TTS voices are unavailable commercially, text-to-phoneme synthesis models trained on locally collected speech data could be developed as open-source alternatives.

### 5.5.2 Integration with Government Agricultural Programs

The Federal Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development (FMARD) and various State Ministries of Agriculture operate subsidy, input distribution, and extension programmes that many smallholder farmers are unable to access due to information gaps. Future work should investigate formal integration between FarmBuddy and government programme registries — for example, the Agricultural Transformation Agenda Support Programme (ATASP) or the Anchor Borrowers' Programme (ABP) — to allow the AI to proactively notify farmers of programmes they qualify for, assist them in completing application forms through a guided conversational flow, and track their application status. Such integration would require formal data-sharing agreements with government agencies and the development of secure API connections to government databases, but could substantially amplify the system's impact by connecting farmers to existing institutional support structures they are currently unaware of.

### 5.5.3 IoT Sensor Integration for Real-Time Farm Monitoring

The advisory capability of FarmBuddy is currently limited by the fact that its awareness of farm conditions depends entirely on what the farmer chooses to report. A significant enhancement would be the integration of low-cost IoT sensors deployed in the field — including soil moisture sensors, soil temperature probes, leaf wetness sensors, and solar radiation meters — that transmit readings directly to the farmer's FarmBuddy profile via a microcontroller and GPRS module. With sensor data automatically updating the farm's environmental context, the AI model could proactively alert farmers when soil moisture drops below a crop-specific threshold, when temperature conditions favour the emergence of specific pests or fungal diseases, or when accumulated heat units indicate that a crop is approaching a critical phenological stage. Research in this direction should explore appropriate, cost-effective sensor hardware (such as the ESP32 or Arduino-based solutions) and investigate LPWAN communication protocols (LoRa, NB-IoT) that can sustain sensor connectivity in areas with poor mobile coverage.

### 5.5.4 Machine Learning Model Fine-Tuning on Local Data

The plant disease detection component of FarmBuddy currently relies on the general-purpose Gemini Vision model, which was trained predominantly on globally distributed agricultural image datasets with limited representation of Nigerian crop varieties, disease pressures, and photographic conditions. Future work should pursue the collection of a large-scale, locally sourced image dataset of Nigerian crop diseases — in collaboration with FMARD, state agricultural development programmes, and university agronomy departments — that captures the specific visual presentations of diseases in Nigerian field conditions (including characteristic soil backgrounds, lighting, and leaf morphologies). This dataset should be used to fine-tune a specialised vision model or to train a classification head on top of a pretrained backbone using transfer learning. A locally fine-tuned model would be expected to substantially outperform the general-purpose baseline, particularly for diseases that are prevalent in Nigeria but underrepresented in global datasets. The fine-tuned model should subsequently be evaluated against the general-purpose Gemini Vision baseline on a held-out Nigerian test set to quantify the improvement and justify the data collection effort.
